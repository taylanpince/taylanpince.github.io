I came to this book backwards. Years before reading it I listened to every
episode of the podcast it came out of, and that was during COVID, when we were
still living in Toronto. I'd go out for night walks in the middle of a frigid
Canadian winter with John Green in my ears. His voice is sorrowful and calming at
the same time, which turned out to be roughly what I needed that year.

So reading the book was mostly nostalgia. I should say that up front, because it
probably inflated my rating.

## What it is

Short essays about pieces of the human-centered world, things like Halley's
Comet, Diet Dr Pepper, the QWERTY keyboard and sunsets, each ending with a rating
out of five stars. The conceit is tongue in cheek and I don't think Green takes it
particularly seriously either. It's a frame to hang the essays on, and the essays
are the point.

## Highlights from the book

Looking back at what I marked, almost all of it is about loneliness. That wasn't
deliberate, but it isn't surprising either. I read it from that angle, and I
think Green writes from that angle most of the time.

The one I've repeated to the most people is about bears. Northern Europeans
avoided saying their actual word for bear, possibly because naming one might
summon it, and the avoidance worked so completely that the original word is gone
from the language. What's left is the replacement. We've been calling them "You
Know Who" for a thousand years and mostly haven't noticed.

Then there's Green trying to comfort a friend and failing:

> They won't be okay, of course, but they will go on, and the love you poured
> into them will go on. That's what I should've said. But what I actually said,
> while crying, was, "How can this be happening? You do so much yoga."

I find that one very funny and very sad, and I don't think it works without both.

There's a definition of personhood in here that I keep thinking about. The
responsibility of being a person is recognizing personhood in others, listening
to their pain and taking it seriously even when you can't feel it yourself. It's
a demanding standard. It's also just a description of listening properly, which
is harder than it sounds and which I'm not always good at.

The one that landed most personally is Donald Hall, writing after the death of
his wife, on what he called third things:

> We did not spend our days gazing into each other's eyes. We did that gazing
> when we made love or when one of us was in trouble, but most of the time our
> gazes met and entwined as they looked at a third thing.

Hall's examples are objects, habits, arts, institutions, games, children. I think
this is a genuinely useful way to look at a marriage and it rings true for mine.
You're not looking at each other most of the time. You're looking at the same
third thing together, and having good ones matters.

And one more, mostly because it made me laugh: Green quotes Matthew on it being
easier for a camel to pass through the eye of a needle than for a rich man to
enter the Kingdom of God, and a minister pointing out that people take every line
of the Bible literally except that one, which is the only line actually meant
literally.

## Stable communities

The passage I've thought about most in a work context is Green quoting Vonnegut,
writing back in 1974:

> What should young people do with their lives today? Many things, obviously. But
> the most daring thing is to create stable communities in which the terrible
> disease of loneliness can be cured.

A large part of my job is building cohesion out of people who live in different
countries, speak different native languages, sit in different time zones and
carry different cultural expectations about what work even is. I've
[written about remote teams before](/post/on-remote-teams/), mostly about tooling
and process, which is the easy part to write about.

Vonnegut is describing the hard part. You can get the tooling right and still end
up with a group of people who are individually fine and collectively nothing. The
daring thing isn't the coordination, it's the community, and I don't think our
industry has gotten appreciably better at it since 1974.

## Podcast vs. book

The podcast is better. The essays were written to be read aloud by the person who
wrote them, and something goes missing on the page. If you haven't encountered
either, start with the podcast and treat the book as a way to go back to the
episodes you liked.
